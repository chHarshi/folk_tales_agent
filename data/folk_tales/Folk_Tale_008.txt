**Folk Tale 008**

**Region:** Rajasthan
**Theme:** Wisdom

In the heart of Marwar, a land painted in hues of sand and ancient stone, reigned Raja Mahendra, a monarch renowned for his fairness and dedication to his people. His kingdom, though prosperous, often grappled with the relentless sun and the scarcity of water. During one particularly harsh drought, the lifeblood of the villages dwindled to a trickle. In the small village of Dhani, two farmers, Ramu and Kishan, shared a single well, a precious communal resource that had sustained their families for generations. As the drought intensified, the well’s water level plummeted, becoming a source of immense anxiety for all.

One scorching morning, the villagers awoke to a strange sight. The shared well, which had been completely dry the day before, now held a substantial pool of clear water – but only on Ramu’s side. Ramu, with a triumphant gleam in his eyes, declared it a miracle, a divine blessing bestowed upon him for his piety. Kishan, however, a man of simple honesty, felt a knot of suspicion tighten in his stomach. How could water reappear so selectively in a shared well? He argued with Ramu, who merely scoffed, claiming Kishan was jealous of his good fortune. The dispute soon reached the ears of Raja Mahendra, who knew that justice in matters of water was paramount to survival.

Raja Mahendra summoned both farmers to his grand court. His most learned ministers and astute advisors meticulously inspected the well, questioned other villagers, and debated legal precedents, but found no conclusive evidence. The well itself offered no secrets, and Ramu’s story of a miracle, while convenient, couldn't be disproven. The Raja, accustomed to solving complex problems, found himself at a crossroads. The drought raged, and the well’s water, claimed by one, was desperately needed by all. The suffering of his people weighed heavily on his heart, and he knew a decision, a wise one, had to be made swiftly.

In his frustration, a grizzled old guard spoke up, suggesting an unconventional approach. "Your Majesty," he began, "there is an old woman in the hills, Daadi Gulab, whose mind is said to be sharper than any sword. She sees not just with her eyes, but with her heart and spirit." Raja Mahendra, though initially skeptical of an old villager's ability to solve what his court of scholars could not, was desperate. He sent for Daadi Gulab. When she arrived, she was a small, stooped figure, her face a roadmap of wrinkles, yet her eyes held an astonishing depth and clarity.

Daadi Gulab did not approach the well, nor did she ask for scientific explanations. Instead, she sat quietly before the two farmers and asked a single, unusual question. Turning first to Ramu, she inquired gently, "Tell me, my son, with this sudden abundance of water in your possession, what weighs most heavily on your mind these days?" Ramu, without hesitation, replied, "Oh, Daadi, my greatest worry is that Kishan, or others from the village, might try to steal my precious water under the cover of night!" Daadi Gulab then turned to Kishan, asking the same question. Kishan, with tears in his eyes, confessed, "Daadi, my heart aches with fear for my family and my parched fields. My greatest worry is that my crops will wither, and we will all perish without a drop of water."

Daadi Gulab rose slowly, her gaze sweeping over the court, finally resting on Raja Mahendra. "Your Majesty," she declared with unwavering certainty, "the well, in its entirety, belongs to Kishan." A murmur went through the court. "If the water truly returned miraculously to Ramu's side," Daadi Gulab explained, "he would have no fear of theft from a communal resource. He would rejoice in the divine gift, perhaps even share it generously. But his immediate concern is not gratitude or survival, but guarding his 'possession' from a neighbour who has nothing. This fear reveals a guilty conscience, for one only guards what one knows has been unfairly taken or hidden." Ramu, confronted by this profound insight into his very thoughts, broke down and confessed. He had secretly diverted a crucial underground channel to his side of the well when it was dry, intending to claim all future water for himself.

Raja Mahendra, struck by Daadi Gulab’s profound wisdom, ensured Ramu was justly punished and the well’s flow was restored to all. The water, once a source of discord, became a symbol of unity and justice. From that day forth, Raja Mahendra often consulted Daadi Gulab, understanding that true wisdom lay not always in books or power, but often in the unassuming observations and deep understanding of human nature found in the simplest of souls.

**Moral:** True wisdom sees beyond superficial appearances and delves into the heart of the matter, often finding truth in the simplest observations and understanding of human nature.
