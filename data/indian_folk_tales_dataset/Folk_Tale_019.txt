Region: West India
Language: English
Theme: selflessness

## Folk Tale 019

### The Flute in the Well

In the sun-baked plains of West India, nestled amidst thorny bushes and gnarled acacia trees, lay the village of Sukhnagar. Its name, meaning 'city of happiness', once rang true, for its fields of bajra and jowar were bountiful, fed by a deep, communal well. But over many seasons, the rains had grown scarce, the earth cracked into a thousand fissures, and the well, once the lifeblood of the village, had dwindled to a muddy puddle. Despair hung heavy in the air, a thick blanket suffocating the once vibrant spirits of the villagers. Among them lived a humble farmer named Raju, known not for his wealth – for he had none – but for the gentle kindness that shone from his eyes.

One sweltering afternoon, a revered Sanyasi, his face etched with wisdom and his saffron robes dusty from travel, arrived in Sukhnagar. News of his presence spread like wildfire, and soon the entire village gathered around him, their faces pleading. "Oh, wise one," they implored, "our fields are barren, our children thirsty. Tell us, what can save Sukhnagar from this cruel drought?" The Sanyasi closed his eyes in contemplation, then spoke in a voice like the rustling of dry leaves. "The heavens will open, and your well will overflow," he declared, "but only when an act of true, unparalleled selflessness is performed – a sacrifice not of life, but of one's most cherished possession, given purely for the good of all, and known only to the giver."

The Sanyasi's words stirred a flurry of discussion. Villagers pondered what their 'most cherished possession' might be. Some thought of their silver bangles, others their small plots of land, yet others their last handful of coins. But as each person considered giving up their precious item, a little voice of doubt, of self-interest, whispered in their ear: "What if it isn't enough? What will I gain? Who will know of my great sacrifice?" And so, one by one, their noble intentions withered, replaced by practical excuses. Raju, however, had little of worldly value. His sole treasure, his heart's delight, was a small, intricately carved wooden flute, a gift from his late grandfather. Its melodies were his comfort, his joy, and the last tangible link to his beloved family.

That night, as the moon cast long, dancing shadows across the silent village, Raju wrestled with his heart. The flute was not gold or jewels, but to him, it was beyond price. It was his song, his soul. Yet, the Sanyasi's words echoed in his mind: "purely for the good of all, and known only to the giver." If his flute, his dearest possession, could bring rain to his suffering village, then what was its loss compared to the joy of a thousand villagers? With a heavy sigh and a prayer for strength, Raju rose. He walked stealthily to the almost-dry well, his fingers tracing the smooth, familiar wood of his flute one last time. He whispered a silent plea for rain, not for himself, but for every parched throat and barren field, and gently, with a pang that tore at his heart, he placed his beloved flute into the muddy depths, letting it sink without a sound.

The next morning, a hush fell over Sukhnagar. A cool breeze, alien after months of blistering heat, swept through the village. Then, a miracle unfolded. Dark, pregnant clouds, unseen for countless days, began to gather in the sky, swiftly blanketing the merciless sun. A soft drizzle began, tapping lightly on rooftops, then grew into a steady, life-giving downpour. It rained for days, soaking the thirsty earth, filling the well to its brim, and washing away the villagers' despair. Sukhnagar rejoiced, attributing the blessing to the Sanyasi's prophecy and the unknown act of selflessness. The Sanyasi, before departing as mysteriously as he arrived, met Raju's gaze, a knowing smile playing on his lips. Raju, his heart full with the sight of his flourishing village, simply smiled back, needing no recognition for his silent sacrifice.

Sukhnagar thrived once more, its fields green and its well ever-flowing. Raju continued his simple life, tending his reborn fields, a quiet peace residing within him. Though he no longer had his flute, the music of the village's renewed laughter filled his soul. The Sanyasi’s riddle, though never fully solved by the villagers, left a profound lesson in its wake.

**Moral:** True selflessness is an act performed without expectation of reward or recognition, solely for the benefit of others. It is the purest form of giving, and it holds the power to bring about the greatest good.
